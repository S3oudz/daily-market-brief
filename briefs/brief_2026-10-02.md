## 9.5 What the Headlines Missed

**What the headlines said:** This week's coverage of the soft August core PCE print (released
Wednesday) widely framed it as lowering the odds of an October rate hike — one tracker cited in
Wednesday's brief (FXEmpire) put implied October hike odds at roughly 35%, down from 68–77%
reported Tuesday, and multiple outlets described the print as easing pressure on the Fed to
keep raising rates.

**What the primary document actually says:** The Federal Reserve's own September Summary of
Economic Projections — published at the same September meeting where it raised the federal
funds rate to a 3.75%–4.00% target range — shows **16 of 18** FOMC participants project at
least one more quarter-point hike before year-end 2026, with a median year-end-2026 rate
projection of **4.10%**, up from the prior quarter's 3.80% projection (multiple sources
summarizing the Fed's published SEP, via search). That projection was set *before* this week's
PCE print and, as of this writing, has not been revised by the Fed itself — the only thing that
has moved is market-implied probability derived from fed-funds futures pricing, which is a
market bet on what the Fed will do, not a statement from the Fed about what it plans to do.

**The gap:** Headlines treating "soft PCE print → falling rate-hike odds" as equivalent to "the
Fed is turning less hawkish" conflate two different things: a market-derived probability that
reprices daily with every data point, and the Fed's own stated projection framework, which only
updates at FOMC meetings (next one October 28) and currently still points to another hike. A
reader who only saw this week's "hike odds plunge to 35%" headlines would reasonably conclude
the Fed itself has gotten more dovish; the primary document says the committee's own median
view, as of its last official projection, has not. **Label: FACT** on the SEP numbers (16 of
18 participants, 4.10% median) and the September rate decision; **PREDICTION/my read** that
this gap matters because it sets up a real test on October 28 — if the Fed holds or signals a
pause despite its own September dot plot, that would be the more newsworthy shift; if it hikes
as the dot plot still suggests, that would actually be the "no surprise" outcome that some of
this week's market-odds-driven coverage has undersold.

💡 *What it means:* **Fact:** two legitimate, differently-sourced numbers (a futures-market
probability and the Fed's own dot plot) are both currently in circulation and point in
different directions. **Inference (medium confidence):** the market-implied odds are more
likely to be the one that moves again before October 28 (they reprice on every data point),
while the dot plot is fixed until the Fed updates it — so treat the "35% chance of a hike" framing
as a snapshot of market sentiment this week, not a prediction that supersedes the Fed's own
stated plan.

➡️ *What you could do:* Going into October 28, don't anchor on whichever single hike-odds number
is most recently reported — check it specifically against the 4.10% year-end median from the
Fed's own September SEP, since a hike that would "surprise" a reader following only market-odds
headlines would not be a surprise relative to the Fed's own most recent official projection.

### Glossary
- **Basis point (bp)** — 1/100th of a percentage point.
- **FOMC** — the Federal Open Market Committee, the Fed body that sets interest-rate policy.
  Next meeting October 28; September meeting minutes due October 7 (Sections 4, 9.5, 11).
- **Summary of Economic Projections (SEP) / "dot plot"** — the Fed's quarterly chart showing
  each policymaker's individual projection for where interest rates should be; distinct from
  market-implied rate-hike odds derived from futures prices, which update daily (Section 9.5).
- **CME FedWatch / rate-hike-odds trackers** — tools that convert fed-funds futures prices into
  implied probabilities of a rate move at the next FOMC meeting; this brief has repeatedly found
  meaningful disagreement across trackers (Sections 9.5, 10) — read the direction of a move as
  more reliable than the precise percentage, and check it against the Fed's own dot plot.
- **Core PCE (Personal Consumption Expenditures) price index** — the Fed's preferred inflation
  gauge; August's reading (+0.2% m/m, +3.0% y/y) was released Wednesday, September 30 and is the
  print behind this week's hike-odds debate (Sections 4, 9.5).
- **Continuing resolution (CR)** — a stopgap funding bill that keeps the government open at
  prior-year funding levels without a full annual budget; the current CR (H.R. 6500, signed
  September 2, 2026) funds the government through December 11, avoiding an October shutdown
  (Section 1, 11).
- **ADP National Employment Report** — a private payrolls estimate released ahead of the
  government's official nonfarm payrolls report; the two frequently diverge and ADP is best
  read as a rough preview, not a substitute (Sections 1, 11).
- **Put option** — a contract that gives the holder the right to sell a stock at a set price;
  buying puts is a way to bet a stock will fall while capping the maximum loss to the premium
  paid, unlike a short sale which carries theoretically unlimited risk (Section 10).
- **VIX** — the "fear gauge"; the options market's expectation of S&P 500 volatility over the
  next 30 days. Below 16 = calm; above 25–30 = genuine stress. Currently 16.39 (Section 3).
- **AAII survey** — a weekly poll of individual (retail) investors on bullish/bearish
  sentiment; the bull-bear spread (bulls minus bears) is the standard summary metric. Moved to
  −11.9 for the week ending September 30, from −15.4 the prior five weeks (Section 3).
- **WTI vs. Brent crude** — two different crude oil benchmarks; WTI is the US domestic
  benchmark, Brent is the international (North Sea) benchmark. Both rose Thursday on China's
  fuel-export suspension, though by different magnitudes (Section 7).
