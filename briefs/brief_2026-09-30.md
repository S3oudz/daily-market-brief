## 9.5 What the Headlines Missed

**What the headlines said:** Coverage of Oura's IPO through late last week emphasized the deal
being "about four times oversubscribed" (Bloomberg, Sept 25) — language that reads, on its
face, as strong investor demand for the listing.

**What the actual deal mechanics say:** Oura still pulled the IPO entirely on Tuesday, hours
before pricing (TechCrunch, CNBC, Axios, NPR). Reporting on the withdrawal clarifies why the
oversubscription number was misleading as a standalone demand signal: the "4x" figure counted
orders across the whole $40–44 range, but a large share of that demand sat below the top of
the range — investors wanted in, but mostly not at the price Oura and its underwriters wanted
to sell at. That mattered more than usual here because 73% of the shares on offer were being
sold by early investors and insiders (via Olympus Partners-style selling shareholders, per
search) rather than the company itself — sellers cashing out have every incentive to hold for
$44 rather than accept a lower clearing price, so a deal can be "oversubscribed" in aggregate
and still fail to clear at the marketed range.

**The gap:** "4x oversubscribed" was accurate but incomplete — it described the volume of
interest, not the price at which that interest existed. A reader relying on the oversubscribed
headline alone would have expected a successful, likely upsized pricing; what actually happened
was a full withdrawal. **Label: FACT** on the oversubscription reporting, the withdrawal, and
the 73%-secondary-shares detail; **PREDICTION/my read** that this pattern — high aggregate
demand, insufficient demand at the top of the range, heavy insider selling — is a more reliable
early-warning sign for a pulled IPO than the oversubscription multiple itself, and worth
checking on future high-profile listings (including Anthropic's, whenever it prices).

💡 *What it means:* **Fact:** an "oversubscribed" IPO can still fail to price if the demand is
concentrated at the bottom of the range while the sellers need the top. **Inference (medium
confidence):** that distinction — demand volume versus demand price — is the detail most
retail-facing coverage skipped, and it's the detail that actually predicted Tuesday's outcome.

➡️ *What you could do:* On future IPO coverage, look past the oversubscription multiple to the
seller mix (primary company shares vs. secondary/insider shares) — a deal that's mostly
insiders selling into a range is structurally more fragile than one where the company is
raising primary capital, regardless of how oversubscribed the headline number looks.

  of a rate hike or cut at the next FOMC meeting; different trackers currently show 68–77% for

- **PCE (Personal Consumption Expenditures) price index** — the Fed's preferred inflation
  gauge, released monthly by the Bureau of Economic Analysis. August data lands today,

- **JOLTS** — the Job Openings and Labor Turnover Survey, a monthly Bureau of Labor Statistics

- **Consumer Confidence Index (Conference Board)** — a monthly survey of how US households

  within a year (Section 4).
- **Oversubscribed (IPO)** — when investor demand for an offering exceeds the number of shares
  available; a high multiple describes demand *volume*, not necessarily demand at the *price*
  the seller wants, which is why an oversubscribed deal can still be withdrawn (Section 9.5).
- **ISM Manufacturing PMI** — a monthly survey-based reading of US factory activity; above 50

- **Strait of Hormuz** — the narrow shipping channel between Iran and Oman through which
  Middle East oil exports transit; flows have recovered toward pre-war levels as of this week

- **VIX** — the "fear gauge"; the options market's expectation of S&P 500 volatility over the
  next 30 days. Below 16 = calm; above 25–30 = genuine stress. Currently 16.04 (Section 3).
- **AAII survey** — a weekly poll of individual (retail) investors on bullish/bearish
  sentiment; the bull-bear spread (bulls minus bears) is the standard summary metric.
  Unchanged at −15.4 for a fourth week; next update Thursday, October 1 (Section 3).
- **Statutory maximum penalty** — the highest fine a law allows per violation; a jury or
  regulator can find the number of violations, but a judge sets the actual per-violation
  amount, often well below the statutory ceiling (carried from prior briefs).
