## 9.5 What the Headlines Missed

**What the headlines said:** Sunday and Monday's coverage (Fortune, Bloomberg, FX Leaders) framed
Trump's announcement as a fresh, market-moving de-escalation breakthrough — "outlines of an emerging
deal," "largely negotiated" — and oil/futures markets reacted as if the Hormuz crisis is
meaningfully closer to resolution.

**What the primary chronology actually shows:** This is not the first time a Hormuz reopening "deal"
has been announced since the war began February 28, 2026. On April 7, Trump announced a two-week
ceasefire with Iran agreeing to reopen the strait (Al Jazeera). Separately, Trump and Iran's
president signed what was reported as a 14-point deal to end the war, open the strait, and ease
sanctions (NBC News). In late June, Trump and Iranian President Pezeshkian signed a memorandum of
understanding laying out terms for reopening the strait, including 60 days of free safe passage
followed by negotiations with Oman over the route's future administration. None of these fully
held: the strait has continued to see attacks since, most recently the LNG tanker struck by a
cruise missile on Saturday, August 1 — one day *before* this newest "deal" was announced.

**The gap:** Each announcement has been covered by financial media as a discrete, fresh
market-moving event — and each time, oil has moved sharply on the news. What the coverage
consistently underweights is the base rate: this is at minimum the fourth reported
ceasefire/deal/MOU covering the same terms (reopen the strait, end hostilities) in five months, and
at least three prior versions did not hold beyond a few weeks. That doesn't mean this one won't —
but "another deal announced" carries less information than the market's 7% oil move suggests, given
how often the same headline has appeared and not stuck.

💡 *What it means:* **Fact:** at least four distinct ceasefire/deal announcements covering Hormuz
reopening have been reported since February 2026 (April 7 ceasefire, an NBC-reported 14-point deal,
the late-June MOU, and this weekend's outline), and attacks have recurred after each of the first
three. **Inference (medium confidence):** the market's willingness to move oil 7% on each new
announcement — rather than pricing in a discount for the pattern's track record — suggests either
that each deal genuinely contains new, more credible terms than the last, or that oil is trading
headline-to-headline without fully accounting for the recurring-announcement pattern. Distinguishing
between those two readings requires watching whether *this* deal survives longer than 60 days (the
June MOU's stated safe-passage window), not just whether oil reacts to the announcement itself.

➡️ *What you could do:* Before trusting any single "Hormuz deal reached" headline as a reason to
stay short oil or long risk assets, check how long the *previous* deal lasted before the next
incident (in this case, the June MOU's terms were followed by a fresh attack within about five
weeks). If this deal breaks the same way, the pattern — not the headline — is the more reliable
guide to how long any given oil-price relief lasts.

- **AAII survey** — weekly poll of retail investors on whether they're bullish/bearish for the next 6 months.
- **FOMC (Federal Open Market Committee)** — the Fed's rate-setting committee; votes on the benchmark federal funds rate at each meeting, and dissents (votes against the majority decision) signal internal disagreement about the right policy path.
- **10-year / 30-year Treasury yield** — the interest rate the US government pays to borrow over those terms; rising yields raise borrowing costs economy-wide and increase the discount rate applied to future corporate earnings, which pressures high-multiple growth stocks most.
- **Brent vs. WTI** — the two main global oil benchmarks; Brent is the international reference price, WTI is the US domestic one. They usually move together but can diverge on regional supply/demand shocks.
- **ISM Manufacturing PMI** — a monthly survey-based index of US factory activity; above 50 = expansion, below 50 = contraction.
- **ETF proxy** — an exchange-traded fund used to represent an index or asset (SPY for the S&P, GLD for gold); ETF proxies only trade during US market hours, unlike crypto or oil futures.
- **SOX / SMH** — the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index and its tracking ETF (SMH); the standard benchmark for chip-sector performance.
- **MOU (Memorandum of Understanding)** — a non-binding agreement outlining terms both sides intend to formalize later; weaker than a signed treaty or contract, which is relevant to how much weight to put on any single "deal" headline (Section 9.5).
- **Contract expiry (futures)** — futures contracts (e.g., oil) have fixed expiration dates; when one month's contract expires, trading rolls to the next month's contract, which can trade at a different price.
- **Basis point / bp** — one-hundredth of a percentage point; a 25bp move is 0.25%, the Fed's standard rate-adjustment increment.
