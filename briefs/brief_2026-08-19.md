## 4. The story that matters most

**The Wall Street Journal published an analysis of tech-company financial footnotes showing roughly
$3 trillion of off-balance-sheet AI commitments across nine major firms — about five times their
reported capex — and the entire AI hardware and power complex was sold on it.**

**Fact — the report.** A WSJ analysis of footnote disclosures found that nine top technology
companies carry approximately **$3 trillion in off-balance-sheet AI-related commitments**, against
roughly **$600 billion of reported capital expenditure** over the past reported year. The total
breaks into two buckets: **unstarted leases of roughly $904 billion to $1.2 trillion** — data-centre
space contracted but not yet occupied — and **purchase commitments of roughly $1.52 trillion to
$1.9 trillion** covering long-term agreements for chips, data-centre construction and energy
procurement. Under current lease accounting, a lease that has not commenced does not appear as a
balance-sheet liability. Individual disclosures: **Alphabet reported $811.0 billion** in material
purchase commitments and other contractual obligations as of June 30, 2026, and **Meta reported
$279 billion** in future lease commitments, a 53% increase from the prior quarter.
([Seeking Alpha](https://seekingalpha.com/news/4633674-big-tech-companies-have-3t-in-hidden-ai-expenditures--report),
[Yahoo Finance video](https://finance.yahoo.com/video/wsj-reports-3t-ai-spending-144501122.html),
[Bloomberg](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-23/alphabet-s-future-spending-commitments-soar-to-811-billion),
[Alphabet 10-Q, SEC EDGAR](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0001652044/000165204426000071/goog-20260630.htm))

**Fact — the market reaction.** Memory, storage, optics and power-infrastructure names took the
brunt. From the data bundle: **Coherent −12.81%** ($305.995), **Talen Energy −10.96%** ($317.65),
**Lumentum −9.88%** ($872.90), **Marvell −7.79%** ($216.03), **Western Digital −7.50%** ($495.85),
**Micron −7.24%** ($938.82), **Vertiv −6.88%** ($272.465), **GE Vernova −6.80%** ($1,005.505).
Reporting also cited SanDisk down roughly 9% and Bloom Energy down 10%. Notably, **Nvidia fell only
2.39%** ($219.73). One outlet described the move as profit-taking rather than fundamental news,
noting SanDisk had entered Tuesday up 653% year to date and Micron up 255%.
([24/7 Wall St. — memory](https://247wallst.com/investing/2026/08/18/wsj-report-sends-memory-stocks-down-sandisk-down-9-micron-down-7-western-digital-down-5/),
[24/7 Wall St. — behind-the-meter](https://247wallst.com/investing/2026/08/18/behind-the-meter-energy-stocks-fall-tuesday-ftai-aviation-down-7-ge-vernova-down-6-caterpillar-down-4/))

**Fact — the bond leg, which compounded it.** The **30-year Treasury yield touched 5.327%**, a fresh
19-year high, before easing to close at **5.285%**. The **10-year rose 1.7 basis points to 4.739%**.
Drivers cited: stalled US-Iran talks pushing oil above $90, surging government spending, heavy
long-dated issuance, and inflation that has run above the Fed's target for five years.
([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/08/18/treasury-yields-.html),
[CNBC — three things](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/08/18/30-year-treasury-yield-three-things-that-could-drive-it-even-higher.html))

💡 *What it means:* **Fact:** the WSJ report contains no new information about demand, no downgrade,
no guidance cut and no accounting violation. It is an aggregation of numbers that were already
published in quarterly filings. **Inference (high confidence):** what moved the market was therefore
not the discovery of a liability but the **arithmetic of the ratio** — $3 trillion of contracted
future spend against $600 billion of annual reported capex means the buildout is roughly five years
pre-committed, and every one of those commitments has to be funded out of future cash flow or future
borrowing at whatever the long bond costs when the bill arrives. **Inference (medium-high
confidence) on why the bond leg matters so much here:** a 5.3% 30-year is not merely a discount-rate
problem for AI valuations, it is the *actual financing cost* of $3 trillion of contracted
obligations. Those two stories, which have been running in parallel for weeks, connected on Tuesday.
That is why the reaction was violent even though the underlying facts were old. **Inference (medium
confidence) on Nvidia's relative resilience:** Nvidia fell one-third as much as its suppliers
because Nvidia's revenue is arriving *now* while its suppliers' valuations depend on capex that
exists, so far, as a footnote. The market sold by contract certainty.

➡️ *What you could do:* Go and read the ratio yourself rather than trusting the aggregate — for any
AI-exposed company you hold, open the latest 10-Q on **sec.gov/edgar** and find the note titled
**"Commitments and Contingencies"** (Alphabet's shows $811.0 billion, of which **$200.7 billion is
short-term**). The split between short-term and long-dated is the number that tells you whether an
obligation is a near-term funding problem or a 2030s aspiration, and the $3 trillion headline
deliberately does not carry it. On the bond side, the operative level is unchanged: **5.50% on the
30-year**. What Tuesday added is a reason it now bites twice — once through valuation multiples and
once through the cost of financing commitments already signed.

## 9.5 What the Headlines Missed

Two items, both checkable against primary documents.

### Item one — "hidden" $3 trillion in AI debt is neither hidden nor, mostly, debt

**What the headlines said:** the WSJ analysis was syndicated and aggregated as **"Big Tech companies
have $3T in hidden AI expenditures"** (Seeking Alpha), **"Big Tech holds $3T in off-balance-sheet AI
commitments"** (Crypto Briefing), **"Meta's Balance Sheet Hides $420B in Off-Balance-Sheet AI Debt"**
(Tech Times), and **"WSJ Catches Up, Discovers AI's Off-Balance Sheet Liabilities Are $3 Trillion
And Growing $1.2 Trillion Per Quarter"** (ZeroHedge). The words doing the work are *hidden*, *hides*,
*debt* and *liabilities*.

**What the filings actually say:** **Alphabet's 10-Q for the quarter ended June 30, 2026 discloses
$811.0 billion of material purchase commitments and other contractual obligations, of which
$200.7 billion is short-term**, and states that these relate primarily to technical infrastructure
and inventory under long-term supply agreements and open purchase orders, plus content licences and
**energy take-or-pay contracts**. **Meta's filing discloses $279 billion of future lease
commitments** for data centres, colocations and network infrastructure, with those leases
**commencing between the remainder of 2026 and 2036 and running one to thirty years**. Both figures
appear in the ordinary commitments and lease footnotes that accompany every quarterly filing, in
tables that state the amounts and the periods.
([Alphabet 10-Q, SEC EDGAR](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0001652044/000165204426000071/goog-20260630.htm),
[Bloomberg](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-30/meta-reports-279-billion-in-future-data-center-leases-for-ai),
[Crypto Briefing](https://cryptobriefing.com/meta-279b-ai-data-center-leases/),
[Seeking Alpha](https://seekingalpha.com/news/4633674-big-tech-companies-have-3t-in-hidden-ai-expenditures--report))

**The gap:** three specific distortions, and one real problem the framing obscures rather than
reveals.

1. **"Hidden" is wrong.** These are mandatory disclosures. Lease commitments are required under the
   lease-accounting standard and purchase obligations under the commitments guidance; both sit in
   named footnotes in a document filed with the SEC and published free. Bloomberg reported
   Alphabet's $811 billion on July 23 and Meta's $279 billion on July 30 — three weeks before the
   selloff.
2. **"Debt" is wrong.** A purchase commitment to buy chips, or an energy take-or-pay contract, is a
   contractual obligation to *spend future money on a delivered good*. It carries no principal, no
   interest and no lender. Calling it debt implies a repayment schedule and a creditor that do not
   exist, and it makes the number sound like leverage when it is closer to a backlog of orders.
3. **The aggregate hides the duration.** **$200.7 billion of Alphabet's $811 billion is
   short-term** — the remaining ~$610 billion is not. Meta's leases run to 2036 with terms up to
   thirty years. A single $3 trillion figure spanning a decade compared against one year of capex
   produces a "five times" multiple that is arithmetically true and economically close to
   meaningless.
4. **The genuine problem, which the framing buries:** a lease that has not commenced is **not** a
   balance-sheet liability under current rules, even though the company is contractually bound. That
   is a real accounting gap, it is the thing worth being concerned about, and it means published
   leverage ratios for these companies understate committed future spend. It is also narrower and
   duller than "hidden debt," which is presumably why the louder version travelled.

💡 *What it means:* **Fact:** the market fell roughly 0.7% at the index level and up to 12.8% in
individual names on the aggregation of numbers that had already been published and reported.
**Inference (high confidence):** this is a repricing driven by *salience*, not information. The
disclosures existed; what changed is that someone summed them and gave the sum a frightening name.
**Inference (medium confidence):** that has a practical implication for how you read the next few
weeks. If the move was caused by salience rather than by facts, it can reverse just as fast when
attention moves on — which is consistent with Monday and Tuesday being mirror images. It also means
the *next* genuinely new information on this thread will be the Q3 10-Qs in late October, where the
quarter-over-quarter change in these same footnotes is the thing to read.

➡️ *What you could do:* Build the habit that would have made Tuesday a non-event for you. For each
AI-exposed name you own, once a quarter open the 10-Q on **sec.gov/edgar**, use the filing's own
table of contents to jump to **"Commitments and Contingencies"** and the **lease** note, and record
two numbers: the **total commitment** and the **short-term portion**. That takes a few minutes per
company and it means the next aggregation headline tells you nothing you did not already know. The
specific thing to watch in October: whether Meta's future lease commitments grow another ~50%
quarter over quarter (they went up 53% in Q2) — an accelerating footnote is the real signal, and the
level is not.

### Item two — the Strait of Hormuz is effectively closed, and the price is behaving as though it might be

**What the headlines said:** Reuters, carried in this brief's own data bundle, ran **"Oil edges up
on uncertainty over exports through Hormuz."** Coverage generally frames the situation around the
*expiry of the memorandum of understanding* and *uncertainty* about future exports, with oil "edging"
higher.

**What the physical tracking data actually shows:** transits through the strait have fallen to
roughly **3 to 4 commercial crossings per day, down about 90% from pre-crisis levels**. Over one
recent weekend, **five commodity vessels transited on the Saturday and none at all on the Sunday,
against 31 the previous weekend**. Approximately **3,200 ships — including roughly 800 oil tankers
and cargo carriers — are backed up and idling west of the waterway**, and 65 ships that entered
during the calmer period have not left. Trackers describe the strait as **effectively closed to
commercial shipping as of August 18**, with crude and major product shipments "virtually
non-existent." On the supply-buffer side, the **US Strategic Petroleum Reserve fell 5.3 million
barrels to 293.4 million** in the most recent week — **the lowest level since December 1982** — after
a 6.1-million-barrel draw the week before to 298.7 million.
([Lloyd's List Intelligence](https://www.lloydslistintelligence.com/resources/blog/strait-of-hormuz-brief-5-august-2026),
[Windward](https://insights.windward.ai/),
[BOE Report / DOE data](https://boereport.com/2026/08/17/oil-stocks-in-us-strategic-petroleum-reserve-fall-by-5-3-million-barrels-to-lowest-level-since-1982/),
[EIA Weekly Petroleum Status Report](https://www.eia.gov/petroleum/supply/weekly/))

**The gap:** the word **"uncertainty."** There is no uncertainty about whether exports are flowing
through Hormuz — the tracking data says they have essentially stopped, and have been stopped for
long enough to strand 3,200 vessels. What is uncertain is how long it lasts and how much stored and
alternative-route supply covers the shortfall. Those are very different statements, and only the
second supports "oil edges up." **Brent at roughly $91.3, up 0.5%, is a price consistent with a risk
premium on a possible disruption, not with a realised one.** Meanwhile the buffer that is bridging
the gap is being consumed at 5–6 million barrels a week from the lowest US reserve level since 1982.

💡 *What it means:* **Fact:** two official/commercial datasets — vessel tracking and DOE reserve
levels — describe a supply interruption already in progress and a buffer draining at a measurable
weekly rate. **Inference (medium-high confidence):** the reason the price is not higher is that the
shortfall is currently being covered from inventory rather than from production, and inventory
coverage is finite and quantifiable in a way that geopolitical uncertainty is not. **Inference
(medium confidence, and I want to be careful here):** I am not predicting an oil spike. Commercial
crude inventories actually *rose* 17.4 million barrels in the week to August 7, alternative pipeline
routes exist, and OPEC spare capacity outside the Gulf is real. The defensible claim is narrower and
still useful: the market is pricing this as a *risk* while the shipping data says it is a *fact*, and
that gap can only close in one of two ways — traffic resumes, or the price catches up to the
physical situation.

➡️ *What you could do:* Read the two free primary sources yourself and stop relying on oil headlines
for this. The **EIA Weekly Petroleum Status Report** publishes **Wednesdays at 10:30am ET at
eia.gov** — today — and the SPR line plus commercial crude inventories are on the summary page. The
number that matters is the **weekly SPR draw rate**, which has now run 6.1 and 5.3 million barrels
in consecutive weeks; at that pace the reserve is measured in months. Pair it with any public
Hormuz transit tracker for the daily crossing count. The combination that would say the price is
about to catch up to the physics is **transits staying near zero while commercial crude inventories
turn from build to draw** — that is the point at which stored barrels stop covering the gap. That is
a far better-informed position than waiting for the next "oil edges up" headline, and both inputs
are free.

### Glossary
- **VIX** — the "fear gauge"; the options market's expectation of S&P 500 volatility over the next 30 days. Below 16 = calm; above 25 = stressed. It measures *index* volatility, so it can stay low while individual sectors move violently in offsetting directions.
- **Dispersion** — the gap between the best- and worst-performing parts of a market. High dispersion with low index volatility means money is rotating rather than leaving.
- **AAII survey** — a weekly poll of individual (retail) investors on whether they are bullish or bearish about the next six months, published Thursdays. Historical averages: 37.5% bulls, 31.5% bears.
- **30-year Treasury yield** — the interest rate the US government pays to borrow for 30 years. It matters to stocks because it sets the discount rate applied to profits expected far in the future, so it hits high-growth, long-payoff companies hardest.
- **Basis point (bp)** — one hundredth of a percentage point. A move from 5.31% to 5.285% is about 2.5 basis points.
- **Off-balance-sheet commitment** — a contractual obligation to spend money in future that accounting rules do not require you to show as a liability today. Purchase commitments and leases that have not yet started are the two main kinds.
- **Purchase commitment** — a binding agreement to buy goods or services later (chips, construction, energy). It is an obligation but not borrowed money: there is no principal, no interest and no lender.
- **Take-or-pay contract** — an energy or supply contract where you pay for a committed volume whether or not you use it. Common in data-centre power procurement.
- **Lease commencement** — the point at which a lease starts and, under current accounting standards, first appears on the balance sheet as a liability. Signed-but-uncommenced leases are legally binding but invisible on the balance sheet, which is the genuine gap behind the "$3 trillion" headline.
- **10-Q / "Commitments and Contingencies"** — the quarterly SEC filing, and the specific footnote inside it that lists purchase obligations and other contractual commitments, with a short-term/long-term split. Free at sec.gov/edgar.
- **Behind-the-meter (BTM)** — power generation connected directly to a customer such as a data centre rather than through the public grid. The BTM basket (Talen, Vistra, Constellation, GE Vernova, Vertiv, Bloom) is the power-side AI infrastructure trade.
- **Contract certainty** — how much of a company's future revenue is locked in by signed agreements versus dependent on a customer's announced intentions. Tuesday's selloff sorted the AI complex almost exactly along this line.
- **FOMC minutes** — the detailed record of a Fed meeting, published three weeks after it. Because they show how individual participants argued, they often move markets more than the original decision did.
- **Dissent (FOMC)** — a vote against the committee majority. Three same-direction dissents in July 2026 (Hammack, Kashkari, Logan, all for a hike) was the most since September 2016.
- **Section 338 (Tariff Act of 1930)** — a rarely used authority letting the president impose additional duties on countries found to discriminate against US commerce. Unlike several other tariff powers it has no time limit, and USMCA origin does not exempt covered goods.
- **DRAM / NAND / HBM** — the three main memory chip types. DRAM is working memory; NAND is storage; **HBM (High Bandwidth Memory)** is stacked, very fast memory beside an AI accelerator and the current binding constraint on AI server production.
- **DDR5 RDIMM** — the enterprise/server form of DRAM; its contract price is the standard benchmark for what data-centre memory costs.
- **Pricing power** — the ability to raise prices without losing volume. Apple raising iPhone prices into a memory shortage, and rising on the day, is the observable version of it.
- **Hedonic adjustment (CPI)** — the statistical method that treats a better product at the same price as a price *decrease*. It is why consumer electronics has been a persistently deflationary CPI component, and why a component-driven price rise there would be unusual.
- **SPR (Strategic Petroleum Reserve)** — the US government's emergency crude stockpile, reported weekly by the DOE/EIA. Statutory minimum 252.4 million barrels; DOE has said the true operating floor is around 70 million. At 293.4 million it is the lowest since December 1982.
- **Brent vs. WTI** — the two main oil benchmarks. Brent is the international reference price; WTI the US domestic one.
- **ETF proxy** — an ETF used to stand in for an index or commodity (SPY for the S&P 500, GLD for gold, USO for oil). USO holds front-month futures and loses value to roll costs, so it tracks daily oil moves but is a poor long-term holding.
- **SMH** — the main semiconductor ETF and the standard benchmark for chip-sector performance.
- **Primary vs. secondary shares (IPO)** — primary shares are newly issued and the company keeps the money; secondary shares are sold by existing owners and the company gets nothing. A mostly-secondary deal is an exit, not a fundraise.
- **Lockup / share unlock** — a post-IPO period during which insiders cannot sell. When a tranche expires, tradable supply rises — a mechanical headwind independent of company news.
- **Salience-driven repricing** — a market move caused by attention shifting to information that was already public. Distinguishable from an information-driven move because no new fact entered the world.
